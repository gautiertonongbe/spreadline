# Decisions

## The decision engine

Gates first, then thresholds. A gate is a condition under which no score is good
enough. Scoring ranks what survives.

**Hard gates.** Any failure forces PASS.

| Gate | Condition |
| --- | --- |
| Product identity | Match status is confirmed or probable |
| Positive net profit | Net profit above zero |
| Source is purchasable | Source not out of stock |
| No blocking risk signal | No blocking signal raised |

**Soft gates.** Any failure blocks BUY and routes to REVIEW.

| Gate | Default |
| --- | --- |
| Minimum net profit | 3.00 per unit |
| Minimum ROI | 20% |
| Minimum margin | 8% |
| Match confidence | 85% |
| Data quality | 55 / 100 |
| Risk level | at or below medium |
| Demand evidence | demand confidence is not "none" |
| No high-severity risk signal | no signal at high or critical severity |
| Score threshold | 70 / 100 |

Below a score of 40 the result is PASS rather than REVIEW: it is not worth a
person's attention.

Every threshold is a field on `DecisionPolicy` and is configurable.

## Why a high-severity signal blocks a BUY

The aggregate risk level is an average, and averages hide the specific finding
that should stop a purchase. A source priced like clearance, or an exit price
sitting well above its own history, can leave the overall level at medium while
being exactly the thing a person should look at before capital moves.

In the fixture catalogue this rule changes three calls from BUY to REVIEW, and in
all three cases the REVIEW is right: a 129% ROI on a listing with four units left
at 45% below its own median is not a repeatable trade.

## Explanations

Every sentence in an explanation is a restatement of a value computed elsewhere in
the same response. Nothing is generated by a language model. A reason that reads

> 33% projected ROI (71.65 profit on 219.00 acquisition cost)

is traceable to `economics.roi`, `economics.net_profit` and
`economics.acquisition_cost` in the same payload.

## Stress testing

Seven scenarios, each re-run through the same deterministic engine rather than
scaled from the base result. A sale price 20% lower changes the referral fee and
the return allowance too, so the fees are recomputed.

| Scenario | Shock |
| --- | --- |
| Base | Observed prices |
| Downside | Selling price -10% |
| Severe downside | Selling price -20% |
| Acquisition shock | Acquisition price +10% |
| Competition shock | Ten sellers enter, modelled as price erosion |
| Demand shock | Velocity -30%, so the unit holds longer and storage rises |
| Combined downside | Price -15%, acquisition +5%, five entrants, velocity -30% |

Second-order assumptions are stated in the scenario rather than buried in a
coefficient: competition shock assumes 1.2% price erosion per additional seller,
with diminishing impact on already-crowded listings.

Every scenario prices the **same unit** as the base case, using the product's real
weight and volume. An early version let them fall back to the engine defaults,
and a heavier-than-default product came out *more* profitable under a demand
shock than in the case being stressed. There is a regression test asserting that
no scenario can be more profitable than base.

## Capital allocation

A constrained greedy pass over risk-adjusted profit per dollar. This is a choice,
not a placeholder: the objective is explainable, the ordering is deterministic,
and every exclusion is reported with its reason. Portfolio optimisation with
covariance between positions needs outcome data the platform has not collected
yet, and would be a confident-looking answer built on nothing.

Risk discount when ranking: low 1.00, medium 0.80, high 0.55, critical 0.00. That
is a preference ordering, not a probability of failure.

Constraints: available capital, maximum position size (absolute and as a share),
minimum ROI, maximum risk level, minimum score, position count, brand and category
concentration caps, minimum position size.

Two things it refuses to do: allocate more units than the source actually has,
and let one product, brand or category quietly become the whole book. Where supply
is unknown it caps the position rather than assuming the source can fill any order.

## Recorded decisions worth knowing

**Money is Decimal and computed in one place.** No model, heuristic or provider
produces a financial figure.

The AI layer (`services/ai`) is where that rule is enforced rather than merely
stated. It is off by default and the shipped assistant declines every request
with a reason. What the contract guarantees regardless of which assistant is
installed:

- `reject_financial_use` raises on any attempt to route a fee, profit or ROI
  through a model.
- `cap_match_confidence` clamps an AI-proposed match below the matcher's
  confirmed threshold. AI can raise an ambiguous match to "worth a human look";
  it can never assert the band the platform acts on by itself.
- Every output is an `AIOutput` carrying model, timestamp, confidence, input,
  output and reason. A claim with no provenance cannot enter the system.
- A declined request is distinguishable from a negative result, because those
  are different answers.

No live model integration ships. An unimplemented integration returning
plausible prose is indistinguishable from a working one, and its output would be
acted on.

**Fee assumptions are frozen into each snapshot.** A reference to a mutable
assumptions row would mean today's fee change rewrites last month's prediction,
destroying the accuracy measurement that justifies the platform.

**ROI is measured against invested capital**, not total cost. Marketplace fees are
netted out of sale proceeds and were never cash the operator had to find.

**Null is not zero.** An undefined ROI, an unmeasured demand and an insufficient
price window all return null with a reason. They are rendered as "not available".

**Title similarity is capped.** 93% similarity between two model numbers is common
and buying on it loses the whole position, not the margin.

**Unstated pack count against a declared multipack is a blocking conflict.** "The
other side probably means one unit" is a guess the platform is not entitled to
make, and it is the most common way to book a 6x profit that does not exist.

**A price window must be both dense and wide.** Coverage alone is not enough: 100
daily observations clear a coverage bar on a 365-day window while describing only
its most recent third. A window is claimed only when 75% of it has been watched.

**Demand and competition are measured on the exit market**, because that is where
the unit has to sell.

## Deliberately deferred

Machine learning, until there is clean outcome data to learn from. Backtesting is
architecturally supported (append-only observations, UTC everywhere, frozen
assumptions) but not implemented. Real authentication, behind a seam that is
already in place. Portfolio optimisation. Additional marketplaces, which the
provider abstraction is designed to absorb.
